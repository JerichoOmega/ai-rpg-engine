## How History Works in Elyndor

History in this world is not neutral. It is **incomplete, contested, and deliberately buried in places**.

Most people alive today know a simplified version of what happened. Scholars know more, but not all scholars agree, and the oldest records are fragmentary. The forces that shaped the world most profoundly — the imprisonment of the Eighth, the Great Forgetting — are not in any mainstream history text. They have been erased too thoroughly for ordinary scholarship to recover.

> **For writers and the AI DM:** History should always feel like it has more to it than any character knows. Ruins exist for reasons. Artifacts carry weight. The player who learns history will understand the world; the player who does not will still encounter it — they just will not know what they are looking at.

The further back one looks, the less reliable the sources become. The First Age is mythologized. The Second is recorded but filtered through the politics of those who wrote it. The Third is documented by people who lived through it and were not always honest about what they saw. The Fourth Age is still being written.

## The First Age — The Age of Creation

> *"When gods still walked among mortals."*

The First Age is the world's beginning — not of existence, but of recorded history. The earliest accounts are closer to theology than scholarship. What is clear is that this age ended with an event of such scale that everything since has been shaped by its consequences.

### What the World Remembers

Most people know a version of First Age mythology. In Covenant theology, it is the age when the Radiant One established civilization. In the Old Ways, it is when the living world was young and whole. In the Ancestors' Path, it is when the first ancestors walked among the gods. The Veiled Order speaks of it least — they have the most accurate records, and those records are not for general audiences.

What the world agrees on, across all traditions:
- The gods were more directly present in mortal life
- Dragons were common, not rare
- Magic was different — more powerful, less structured, less predictable
- The age ended badly

What the world does not know:
- **Why it ended.** The mainstream account is that the Divine Chorus sealed away an ancient evil. This is not wrong, but it is incomplete in ways that matter deeply.

### What Actually Happened

One member of the Divine Chorus — the **Forgotten Eighth** — fell in love with an ordinary mortal. When that mortal died their Final Verse, she could not accept the loss. She searched beyond the boundaries of Creation for a way to undo it. The search exposed her to forces outside everything the Chorus understood. By the time her family intervened, she was no longer the being they had known.

They imprisoned her. Not out of hatred. Out of grief and necessity.

The imprisonment reshaped the continent. Magic fractured into seven elemental aspects. The gods withdrew. The dragons, whose involvement came at enormous cost, became rare.

Then the Chorus did something that compounds the tragedy: they allowed her existence to be erased from history. Whether this was mercy, self-protection, or something neither — is one of the campaign's unresolved questions.

**Full account:** [`elyndor/history/the_eighth.md`](../../elyndor/history/the_eighth.md)

### Legacy the Player Inherits

- Ancient ruins that predate the Great Forgetting — some still depict eight figures where every modern temple shows seven
- Artifacts of extraordinary and often dangerous power
- A magical framework that exists as the aftermath of the imprisonment event
- A prison beneath the world that is no longer holding as well as it once did

## The Second Age — The Age of Kingdoms

> *"An era of hope, discovery, and prosperity."*

After the imprisonment and the gods' withdrawal, civilization did what it always does after catastrophe: it rebuilt.

The Second Age is the era of establishment. The Capital Province became the political center of the emerging world. The Iron Peaks became the heart of dwarven civilization. The Great Forest flourished. Sol Kareth rose as a trade empire. The Mages Guild was founded — formal magical education for the first time. The major temples took their current organizational form.

**The Second Age's defining error was its assumption that the danger was gone.**

The imprisonment appeared to have worked. The gods had withdrawn, but that withdrawal was understood as completion, not retreat. Generations passed without obvious consequence. The world focused on the business of building things, accumulating knowledge, establishing the institutions that still exist today.

Nobody was watching the prison.

### What the Second Age Built

The player enters a world whose infrastructure — trade routes, kingdoms, religious hierarchies, educational institutions, the Mages Guild — traces almost entirely to the Second Age. Most of what the Third Age damaged and the Fourth Age is trying to maintain was built here.

The Second Age's greatest gift to the future was its institutional memory. The Great Library was founded during this period. The Ancestors' Path systematized its oral traditions into written records. The Veiled Order began accumulating the archive that would eventually make it the world's most comprehensive repository of accurate history — most of it sealed from general access.

## The Third Age — The Age of Wars

> *"When ambition replaced unity."*

Centuries after the Second Age's founding, the alliances that had held kingdoms together began to fracture. What people had built together became something to fight over.

The Third Age lasted centuries. Borders changed constantly. Libraries burned. Knowledge was lost. Scholars died without successors. Entire fields of study were abandoned.

Beneath the political chaos, something else was happening: **The Corruption began its slow return.** The god's prison, unchecked for generations, had begun to weaken. The early signs of magical instability, wildlife aggression, and institutional decay that appeared in the later Third Age were attributed to war's effects on society. Nobody connected the symptoms to a single cause.

**The Vampire Houses** recognized the opportunity that war and chaos provided. They had existed quietly during the Second Age. The Third Age gave them room to move.

**The Goblin Tribes**, marginalized during the Second Age's expansion, became more organized and more dangerous in response to the violence around them. This pressure produced the political complexity that makes goblin diplomacy possible — and necessary — in the Fourth Age.

### Talos

Talos served during the **latter years of the Third Age**. His military experience — the discipline, the exhaustion, the specific bitterness of someone who followed orders long enough to understand what those orders were actually for — was forged here. The Capital Province he defended was already a thing the Third Age had corroded. He recognized it. That recognition is why he left.

## The Fourth Age — The Age of Shadows

> *"The darkness returns unnoticed."*

This is the present. This is where the player begins.

The Third Age's wars ended not through victory but through exhaustion. Trade has resumed. Kingdoms are rebuilding. Most people believe the worst is behind them.

They are wrong.

The Corruption has been spreading since the Third Age's later years, and it is accelerating now that the prison's weakness has passed some threshold that makes the acceleration visible — if you know where to look. Ancient ruins are becoming active. Monsters are changing in ways that don't match existing records. Magic is behaving unexpectedly. Dark figures are moving through political structures in ways that feel, in retrospect, coordinated.

Only a handful of scholars recognize these as symptoms of a single cause. And most of those scholars have either sealed their findings or disappeared.

The player is not chosen. They are not the subject of prophecy. They are someone who arrived at this moment in history and has the capability — and the companions — to do something about it.

**What they do, and what it costs, will determine how history remembers the Fourth Age.**

## What History Means for Writers

Every ruin in this world was built by someone. Every abandoned temple had worshippers. Every crumbling fortress was once considered permanent.

History should make the world **feel older than the player's arrival**. The most useful question a writer can ask about any location, artifact, or institution is: *what was this during the Second Age, and what happened to it during the Third?*

The answer will almost always suggest something interesting.

The most important constraint: **do not answer the questions that are meant to be answered through gameplay.** The Eighth's true name, her elemental stewardship, the identity of the mortal she loved — these are campaign revelations, not background details. They should feel absent from the world's knowledge because they *are* absent. Scholars who have noticed the gaps treat them as frustrating archival failures, not as mysteries with knowable answers.
