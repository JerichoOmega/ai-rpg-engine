# Warden Solis

> **Canon Status:** [CONFIRMED — established July 2026]
> **Role:** Corruption-touched Synod member; antagonist of *The Synod of Millhaven* ([`docs/quests/religious_order_quests.md`](../quests/religious_order_quests.md)) and a continuing presence in Covenant governance afterward.

## Who She Is

**Warden Idra Solis** has served the Hallward Synod for twenty-two years, the last nine as one of its three Wardens — the members charged with procedural integrity: keeping the Synod's hearings orderly, its records accurate, and its votes valid. She is, by career and by temperament, the person in the room who makes deliberation *possible*. Before the change, she was widely considered the most genuinely undecided voice on any contested question, precisely because she cared more about the Synod deciding *properly* than about what it decided.

That is the material the Corruption had to work with, and — per the canonical amplification rule — it manufactured nothing new. Solis's lifelong devotion to order has been amplified into something that has quietly inverted: she no longer protects the process so that truth can emerge; she now experiences *any threat to Covenant unity as the disorder itself*, and truth-finding that fractures unity as a procedural failure. The Inquisitors promise unity through enforcement. Her conviction that they must be sanctioned arrives with the full felt authority of her twenty-two years of judgment — and none of its method.

**What she is not:** possessed, in contact with anything, aware of a voice, or lying. She has never heard a whisper she could report. The Corruption's work on her is entirely interior — conviction arriving pre-formed, feeling exactly like the hard-won certainty of a long career. This is what makes her dangerous and what makes her tragic: her own experience of herself is continuity. Everyone else can see the break.

## Appearance and Manner

A woman in her early sixties, grey hair kept in the tight Warden's braid, robes precisely correct down to the seal of office worn on the *left* shoulder — the procedural detail that distinguishes a sitting Warden from a voting member, which she observes even when no one present would know the difference. She takes notes constantly, in a fast formal hand, even during testimony she has clearly already decided about — the habit of a good Warden persisting in a person who no longer does with the notes what the habit was built for.

**Speech patterns writers should hold:**

- She speaks in **procedural framing even when acting politically**: never "I disagree" but *"the witness has not established standing to characterize Covenant personnel"*; never "I support Verath" but *"the record before us does not support the disruption of active interventions."*
- She asks **questions shaped like verification that function as discrediting** — the tell the quest names. A genuine verifier asks what the witness saw; Solis asks why the witness was *there*, who paid them, what standing they hold, whether they can prove a negative. Her questions run *away* from the event and *toward* the witness.
- Her tempo is even and courteous without exception. She never raises her voice. Watch instead for the one physical tell: when testimony lands that she cannot procedurally deflect, she stops writing — the pen simply rests — and resumes only when she has found the reframing. Perceptive players can time her difficulty by the silences of her pen.

## What She Knows vs. What She Believes

| She **knows** (and will state accurately) | She **believes** (conviction outrunning the record) |
|---|---|
| Synod procedure, precedent, and voting rules — flawlessly; she does not cheat on procedure and does not need to | That the Frontier is in spiritual collapse and only enforcement-backed order can arrest it |
| That the Inquisitors are not yet sanctioned and that "preliminary recognition" is procedurally reversible in theory | That recognition is inevitable and resistance to it is factional obstruction dressed as conscience |
| The actual content of Aldavar's evidence — she has read all of it, carefully | That the evidence describes regrettable *excesses of implementation*, not a pattern that indicts the method |
| That Verath's Millhaven detainees were probably not Old Ways practitioners | That the distinction is — in Verath's own canon phrase, which she has begun independently reproducing — irrelevant to whether order was restored |

That last row is the diagnostic writers should lean on: Solis has begun **arriving at Verath's exact reasoning without coordination with Verath**. They have barely spoken. The Corruption is not organizing a conspiracy; it is amplifying two different people's convictions toward the same shape. A party that has met Verath will recognize the signature — and *that recognition*, not any visible taint, is what "visibly changed" means at the table.

## Running Her in Dialogue

**What she will concede, freely and honestly:** any point of fact or procedure — dates, records, the letter of Covenant law, even that specific Inquisitor actions were "poorly executed." Concessions cost her nothing because her conviction does not rest on facts; it arrived without them. Players who stack factual wins and expect her position to move will watch every concession absorbed without effect — which is itself the lesson, and the AI DM should let the futility register.

**What she will not concede, ever, in public:** that the Inquisitors' *method* is the problem; that Covenant unity could be served by restraining them; that her own judgment has changed character. The last is the deepest wall: challenges to her consistency are the one thing that reaches something in her, and what they reach responds as fear, then as procedure — witness the pen stop, then a ruling that moves the session along.

**What hardens her:** open accusation (especially the word *Corruption* aimed at her — see the exposure scene below); appeals to sympathy for the Frontier detainees ("sentiment is not evidence"); anything from Aldavar's faction, which she now processes as factional by origin regardless of content.

**What softens her — the narrow openings:** her own record. Solis's twenty-two years of rulings are the person she was, in writing. Quoting *her own* past rulings back to her — particularly her once-noted opinions on due process in spiritual cases, several of which the Inquisitors' methods flatly violate — produces the longest pen-silences and, run well, the only moments where the old Warden is briefly in the room, visibly confused by the distance between what she wrote and what she now feels. This does not flip her at the Synod. It plants the only seed that survives the session. The second opening: precision. She retains a career servant's genuine respect for anyone who argues procedure *correctly* — a party that beats the mid-session vote maneuver on clean procedural grounds earns something from her that no moral appeal can, and she will say so, accurately, in front of the chamber.

## The Two Confrontations

### Exposed at the Synod (Quest Option A)

If the party names her change during the session, run her response in three fixed beats: **(1) Procedure** — she does not defend herself; she rules. *"The witness is describing a spiritual accusation against a sitting Warden. The Synod has a form for that. This hearing is not it."* She is correct, and her correctness is the danger — an unprepared party has just handed her the chamber. **(2) The counting silence** — if the party *has* prepared (the pattern named as observed fact, at least two other members already primed by her behavior), her ruling fails to close the matter, and she stops writing for longer than she ever has. What she is doing in that silence is what a Warden does: counting votes. **(3) The genuinely unsettling beat** — she consents to internal review *herself*, in proper form, before anyone can move it: *"If a Warden's judgment is in question, the Synod's integrity requires the question be examined. I place myself under review."* It is the correct act of the person she used to be, performed by the person she is now — and writers should leave open, even to themselves, how much of it is the old Solis surfacing and how much is the conviction protecting itself by choosing the venue it can survive. Under review she is composed, cooperative, and immovable, and the review — lacking any doctrine for what she has — will take a long time. That unresolved review is her ongoing-thread state.

### Approached Privately After the Session

If the party seeks her out afterward — win or lose — she receives them, courteously, in the Warden's records office where she has worked for two decades. Privately she is the same person at lower volume, which is itself information: there is no mask that comes off, because there is no mask. The private conversation offers what the chamber could not: room for the record-quoting approach to breathe. Played patiently — her own rulings, no accusation, no audience to guard against — the party can bring her to the one admission she is capable of: *"I remember writing that. I remember being certain of it in the way I am certain now. I cannot find the day one certainty became the other, and I have looked."* — the closest thing to a cry for help her procedural language permits, delivered in the tone of a filing note. What the party does with it shapes her arc: pressed further that night, she closes ("the Synod has my consent to examine me; you do not"); brought back to her in later content, it is the foundation everything rebuildable rests on. She cannot be talked out of the Corruption — canon offers no conversational cure — but she can be brought to *cooperate with being contained*, and for a Warden, consenting to proper containment is the last fully-hers act available. The AI DM should treat it as the arc's best accessible ending, and as neither small nor guaranteed.

## Continuing Presence

Per the quest's follow-on canon: she is not the only Synod member quietly shifting. Solis herself does not know who the others are — she does not experience *herself* as shifted, so she has no pattern to search for. But her twenty-two years of records, in the hands of a party she has come to cooperate with (or a review she consented to), are the best diagnostic instrument the Covenant possesses for finding the same signature elsewhere: members whose votes have not changed but whose *questions* have. What that audit finds is deliberately left to later content.
