## Companion Philosophy

**Companions are people, not combat units.**

Every companion has:

- Personal goals
- Beliefs
- Strengths
- Weaknesses
- Friendships
- Opinions
- Fears
- A life outside the player

**The player is not the center of every companion's life.**

Companions travel together because circumstances and shared goals bring them together — not because the player is the protagonist. A companion's first loyalty is to their own values, their relationships with the other companions, and their personal goals. The player earns their trust. They do not automatically have it.

---

## Party Structure

The canonical party system:

- **Six** permanent companions.
- **Four** active party members at any time.
- Companion swapping occurs **only** in civilization and designated safe locations.
- Inactive companions remain part of the larger expedition narratively — they are not absent from the story. They are traveling with the group; they are simply not in the active four.

## Three Companion Pillars

Every companion must have three distinct, authored identities. A companion without all three has not been designed yet.

---

### Pillar One — Combat Identity

How this companion fights. Their role in battle is a direct expression of who they are as a person — not a slot filled by a game designer.

Combat Identity examples:

- Elemental Mage
- Construct Engineer
- Frontline Defender
- Ranger
- Orc Houndmaster
- Werewolf Warrior

A Combat Identity is a complete sentence, not a class name. "Guardian" is a class. "A soldier who learned to absorb punishment so the people he protected didn't have to" is a Combat Identity. The class name is shorthand for the second thing.

---

### Pillar Two — Expedition Identity

How this companion contributes **outside combat**. Every companion must make the player feel that bringing them along changes the journey in meaningful, practical ways.

Expedition Identity examples by confirmed companion:

**Eleanor**
- Magical scholar
- Arcane anomaly identification
- Magical reagent preservation
- Expert on magical phenomena the party encounters

**Torren**
- Master blacksmith
- Salvage and materials expert
- Engineering and structural knowledge
- Equipment efficiency and maintenance

**The Ranger** *(name TBD)*
- Hunter and forager
- Scout and pathfinder
- Survivalist
- Finds food and locates herbs
- Tracks creatures; detects hidden paths; warns of danger ahead

**Healer companion** *(if applicable)*
- Medic and field surgeon
- Herbalist
- Disease recognition and treatment
- Medical knowledge that unlocks options in encounters

**Rogue companion** *(if applicable)*
- Trap detection and disarming
- Lockpicking
- Hidden cache discovery
- Secret passage identification

**Talos**
- Structural and fortification knowledge
- Defensive planning
- Hazard recognition
- Route assessment and safe passage identification

Every companion must contribute meaningfully during exploration and travel, not only during combat.

---

### Pillar Three — Narrative Identity

Every companion brings a unique worldview to the journey.

The defining question for Narrative Identity:

> **"What does this person believe the world needs more of?"**

This belief governs their dialogue, their approval responses, and their personal quest. It is the lens through which they interpret every significant event the party encounters.

Narrative Identity examples:

- Understanding
- Duty
- Compassion
- Freedom
- Craftsmanship
- Patience

A companion's Narrative Identity must produce a specific, recognizable voice. A companion who believes the world needs more understanding will ask questions before drawing conclusions, will push back on snap judgments, and will be specifically troubled by encounters that destroy knowledge rather than just lives. Their Narrative Identity should be traceable in everything they say.

## Companion Approval

**Approval is principle-based. It is not a morality system.**

There is no good/evil axis. There is no alignment. Each companion evaluates the player's decisions according to their own values — values that are specific to who they are as a person. The same player action can gain approval from one companion and lose it from another. Both responses are correct for those companions.

Approval unlocks:

- Additional Journey Conversations
- Companion banter
- Personal quests
- Optional scenes
- Romance (where applicable)

**Companions rarely leave the party.**

Leaving only occurs after repeated, major violations of a companion's most deeply held beliefs through significant player decisions. Disapproval is expressed through conversation, relationship distance, and the absence of unlocked content — not through automatic departure. A companion who disagrees with the player stays and argues. A companion who has been fundamentally betrayed leaves. The threshold is high and must be earned through sustained choices, not a single decision.

---

## Design Principles

Every companion must feel valuable in three contexts:

1. **Combat** — their Combat Identity makes them meaningfully different from every other companion in a fight.
2. **Exploration** — their Expedition Identity changes what the party can discover, access, and understand.
3. **Storytelling** — their Narrative Identity gives them a specific voice in every significant conversation.

**No companion exists solely for damage output.**

A companion who is valuable only in combat has failed all three pillars.

**Every companion should make the player feel that bringing them along changes the journey in meaningful ways.**

Not just "more firepower." Different paths found. Different things understood. Different conversations had. Different choices available.

---

## Future Companion Rule

Before any new companion is approved, it must clearly define all of the following. A companion without a complete answer for every item does not yet exist as a design:

| Requirement | Description |
|---|---|
| **Combat Identity** | How they fight; what makes their combat role specific to them as a person |
| **Expedition Identity** | What they contribute outside combat; how they change exploration and discovery |
| **Narrative Identity** | What they believe the world needs more of; their specific worldview |
| **Two Specialization Paths** | Two philosophically distinct approaches to their Combat Identity (not entirely new identities) |
| **Companion Passive Ability** | One unique ability no other companion shares |
| **Approval Philosophy** | What values they use to evaluate the player's decisions |
| **Personal Quest Theme** | The hidden burden, past failure, personal dream, or unresolved conflict their quest reveals |

Only companions that satisfy all seven requirements should be added to the game.

## Developer Note

The goal of the companion system is to create **believable traveling companions** rather than interchangeable RPG party members.

Players should remember companions because of:

- their personalities,
- their conversations,
- their relationships with each other and with the player,
- their practical contributions to the expedition,
- and the unique philosophies they bring to both combat and the world around them.

**Every companion should feel like someone whose absence would noticeably change both the gameplay experience and the story.**

Not because they are useful in combat. Because they are a specific person who sees the world a particular way and says things nobody else would say and does things nobody else would do.

A companion who could be replaced by a different companion with the same class and role without anyone noticing has failed as a design.
