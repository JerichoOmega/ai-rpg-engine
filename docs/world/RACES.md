## Design Philosophy

Race defines **culture, history, and context** — not personality.

A race should never dictate who a character is as an individual. It provides the world they grew up in, the traditions they were exposed to, the assumptions others may make about them — and the assumptions they may have to fight against.

> Not every dwarf enjoys mining.  
> Not every elf embraces ancient traditions.  
> Not every orc seeks battle.

Individuals are shaped by their experiences, choices, and wounds — not by their ancestry. Racial stereotypes exist within the world as social phenomena that characters navigate. They are not design shortcuts.

This applies to companions as well. Talos is an elf. Ragash is an orc. Neither is defined by what that means to the world around them — they are defined by who they chose to become.

## Playable Races at Launch

Six races are confirmed playable at launch:

| Race | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Humans** | The most widespread race on the continent; culturally diverse; no dominant regional identity |
| **Elves** | Long-lived; deep history; associated with the Great Forest and ancient traditions — but individual elves vary enormously |
| **Dwarves** | Homeland in the Iron Peaks; master craftspeople and miners; underground culture and engineering tradition |
| **Orcs** | Present throughout the continent; own society, culture, and politics — not defined by conflict |
| **Halflings** | Distributed across the continent along river networks and trade roads; the continent's most practiced trading people |
| **Gnomes** | Concentrated in the lower Iron Peaks and the Capital; renowned archivists, instrument-makers, and scholars of recorded knowledge |

### What Each Race Needs (Design Target)

Every playable race must be developed with:

| Element | Description |
|---|---|
| **History** | What this people have been through; major events that shaped them |
| **Traditions** | Practices, values, and cultural norms |
| **Architecture** | What their built environments look like |
| **Values** | What they collectively prize — while individuals may reject these |
| **Culture** | How people live day-to-day; relationship to other races; internal diversity |

What is established for each race:
- Dwarves: homeland in the Iron Peaks; mining and forging tradition
- Elves: Great Forest association; long-lived; ancient history
- Orcs: Ragash confirms they have their own society and traditions; her exile/departure from her clan is a specific story, not a universal one
- Halflings: distributed traders and river people; cultural philosophy of adaptability and hospitality; see [Halflings](#halflings) section below
- Gnomes: archivists and scholars rooted in the Iron Peaks and Capital; cultural philosophy of preservation and documentation; see [Gnomes](#gnomes) section below

> ⚠️ Architecture and detailed day-to-day culture remain NOT YET FULLY DEFINED for Humans, Elves, Dwarves, and Orcs specifically. The elements defined above are confirmed canon; the missing elements are gaps to be filled in future worldbuilding passes.

## Halflings

### Overview

Halflings have no single homeland — they have a continent.

Where other peoples built kingdoms anchored to mountains or forests or deserts, Halflings built a network. Over generations they settled along the continent's river systems, canal junctions, and major trade roads, establishing wayhouses, ferries, market stalls, and family trading houses in nearly every region. The result is a people who are simultaneously everywhere and, by many locals, taken for granted — until the river roads go quiet and everyone realizes how much depended on them.

### Regional Home

Halflings are **distributed**, not concentrated. Every region has halfling communities — the wayhouses on the edge of the Frontier, the river docks feeding into Sol Kareth's desert caravans, the canal-side market quarters of the Capital Province. The heaviest concentration is along the continent's central river corridor, which halfling trading houses have managed for centuries.

There is no "Halfling homeland" in the sense of a sovereign territory. Halfling identity is organized around **family houses** and **trade routes**, not geography.

### Cultural Philosophy — The Road Always Continues

The defining principle of Halfling culture is **the road always continues** — meaning: there is always another path, another approach, another deal to be made. Failure is temporary. A closed door is not a dead end.

This philosophy emerged from centuries of being a small people in a world of larger ones, navigating conflicts and power shifts they could rarely control directly. Adaptability became the supreme virtue.

| Value | Expression |
|---|---|
| **Adaptability** | When one path closes, find another; stubbornness is a failure of imagination |
| **Hospitality** | Every traveler fed and sheltered is a relationship built; hospitality is investment, not charity |
| **Memory** | Halflings remember names, debts, favors, insults, and family ties across generations; their genealogical records are meticulous |
| **Pragmatism** | Halflings make decisions that work, not decisions that sound good; sentiment is respected but never allowed to override the ledger |
| **Discretion** | Information has value; what you do not say cannot be used against you |

### Traditions

**The Laden Table**
Halfling wayhouses and homes maintain the tradition of the laden table — a standing meal available to any traveler who arrives, regardless of station. The table is never described as charity. It is extended as a courtesy between equals. Refusing hospitality without cause is considered a deep insult; accepting it without gratitude is worse.

**House Records**
Every halfling trading house maintains a Book of Memory — a genealogical and commercial record tracking family relationships, significant debts, major transactions, and notable events stretching back as far as the house exists. These books are inherited by the eldest capable child and are among the most prized possessions a halfling can own. A house that loses its Book of Memory has lost part of itself.

**The Farewell Coin**
When a halfling leaves on a long journey — one where return is uncertain — family members press a coin into their palm. Not as currency, but as a token of connection. The traveling halfling carries the coins of everyone who sees them off. Bringing the coins back is proof of survival; the coins are kept, never spent.

**The Reckoning**
Each year, the major halfling trading houses convene at an agreed neutral location (rotating by tradition) to settle outstanding debts, renegotiate agreements, and formalize new partnerships. The Reckoning is more family reunion than business meeting — though the business is real. It is the closest thing halflings have to a continental institution of their own.

### Relations with Other Races and Factions

| Relationship | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Humans** | The most common trading partners; halflings navigate human political shifts by maintaining relationships with multiple factions simultaneously |
| **Dwarves** | A long-established commercial relationship; halfling trading houses have moved dwarven goods out of the Iron Peaks for centuries; mutual respect grounded in shared practicality |
| **Elves** | More distant; halflings find elven timescales frustrating, elves sometimes find halfling networks overly transactional; individual relationships are common; cultural alignment is rarer |
| **Orcs** | Variable; halflings who trade through orc territory learn quickly which communities welcome commerce and which do not; there is no continent-wide relationship, only individual histories |
| **Gnomes** | Generally positive; gnomish archivists and halfling trading houses frequently exchange information and services; gnomes document what halflings move through the world |
| **Adventurers Guild** | Halfling wayhouses often double as informal Guild contact points; a traveling halfling knows where the Guild operates in their region |
| **Mages Guild** | Halfling networks are among the best sources of information on the continent; the Mages Guild has quietly cultivated relationships with several major trading houses |

### Tensions and Internal Diversity

**The Settled vs. the Road**
Halfling communities divide broadly between those who remain mobile — running wagon trains, river ferries, and seasonal circuits — and those who have settled permanently in a city or town. Settled halflings sometimes feel the pull of the road as something lost. Mobile halflings sometimes look at settled communities and see people who gave up. Neither view is universal, and most halflings hold both at once.

**Underestimation as a Cultural Wound**
Halflings are rarely seen as dangerous, threatening, or powerful by other races — and they have learned to use this. But underneath the pragmatic acceptance is a cultural frustration that surfaces in private: the assumption that a halfling in the room is the least significant person in it. Characters from halfling backgrounds carry this in different ways — some as a chip on their shoulder, some as a weapon they wield, some as a wound they've decided not to heal.

**House Rivalries**
The major trading houses compete. Competing on the same routes, for the same contracts, with the same customers — while maintaining the outward courtesy the culture demands — is a halfling art form. House feuds can run for generations while both houses attend the same Reckoning and share the same loaded table.

### Architecture

Halfling-built structures are **low, wide, and oriented toward gathering**. Wide porches and communal spaces at the front; private family quarters at the back. Wayhouses are built to handle more people than they were formally designed for, because the Laden Table means the number of guests is never predictable.

River halfling communities build on or adjacent to the water — docks, floating market platforms, covered bridges that double as market stalls. Everything is designed for throughput: people, goods, and information moving efficiently.

Settled halfling neighborhoods in larger cities are identifiable by their low rooflines, covered walkways between buildings, and the smell of food. They tend to develop around a central wayhouse or market square.

## Gnomes

### Overview

Gnomes are the continent's record-keepers, archivists, and instrument-makers — a people for whom the loss of knowledge is a genuine catastrophe, and the preservation of it a moral imperative.

Their culture emerged from a centuries-long relationship with the dwarves of the Iron Peaks, where gnomes served as the administrative and archival backbone of the underground kingdoms. Over generations, that relationship evolved: gnomes developed their own scholarly traditions, their own institutions, and eventually their own independent communities — though the connection to the Iron Peaks and its culture of craft and permanence remains visible in how gnomes build, what they value, and how they measure a life well lived.

### Regional Home

Gnomes are concentrated in two primary areas:

**The Lower Iron Peaks** — The oldest gnomish communities are carved into the lower reaches of the mountain range, adjacent to (and historically inside) the great dwarven kingdoms. These communities house the most traditional gnomish archival institutions — some of the oldest continuous records on the continent.

**The Capital Province** — A substantial gnomish community has established itself in the Capital, centered around the universities and the Mages Guild's archives. Capital gnomes are more outward-facing than their Iron Peaks counterparts, engaged with the full sweep of continental scholarship and politics.

Smaller gnomish communities exist in Sol Kareth (drawn by the ancient ruins) and scattered through the Frontier (drawn by the practical need for record-keeping in lawless places where contracts are the only reliable law).

### Cultural Philosophy — What Is Not Recorded Is Lost

The defining principle of Gnomish culture is **what is not recorded is lost** — the conviction that knowledge is the only thing that persists through time, and that its preservation is a sacred responsibility.

This belief is not merely academic. Gnomish history includes the destruction of archives — by war, by fire, by deliberate erasure — and the cultural trauma of those losses shapes how gnomes approach everything they do. A gnome who witnesses the destruction of records feels it as a personal wound.

| Value | Expression |
|---|---|
| **Precision** | Approximation is how errors compound; if something is worth recording, it is worth recording correctly |
| **Preservation** | What exists must be protected from loss; the second copy is as important as the first |
| **Thoroughness** | Incomplete records mislead; a gap in the archive is not neutral, it is a lie by omission |
| **Intellectual honesty** | Recording what is inconvenient is as important as recording what is flattering; gnomish archives are trusted specifically because they do not suppress unflattering facts |
| **Craft in service of knowledge** | Gnomish instruments — measuring tools, clocks, lenses, copying machines — are built to serve accurate documentation; aesthetic beauty is secondary to functional precision |

### Traditions

**The Naming of Works**
When a gnome completes a significant creation — an archive, an instrument, a documented discovery — it is formally named in a small ceremony attended by colleagues and family. The name is entered into the relevant archive. A gnome's life is, in part, measured by the names of their works. Unnamed work is work that did not count.

**The Archive Debt**
Every gnome who receives formal education or apprenticeship under an established archivist or institution incurs an Archive Debt — a non-monetary obligation to contribute to the community's collective record. This is discharged through years of work, significant original documentation, or the training of an apprentice who goes on to produce meaningful work. The debt is never fully discharged in a literal sense; it is considered a permanent relationship between the individual and the institution of knowledge itself.

**The Second Copy**
Gnomish archivists never keep only one copy of a significant record. The second copy is kept in a different location — ideally a different building, often a different city. This practice was formalized after historical archive losses. Gnomes who are cavalier about backups are quietly considered careless.

**Apprenticeship and the Lineage of Knowledge**
Gnomish scholars trace their intellectual lineage the way halflings trace their family lineage — a chain of teacher-to-apprentice connections reaching back through generations. Being able to name your full intellectual lineage is a mark of belonging to the scholarly tradition. Gnomes who are self-taught are respected for their work but sometimes lack the social footing that lineage confers.

### Relations with Other Races and Factions

| Relationship | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Dwarves** | The oldest and deepest relationship; gnomes and dwarves built the Iron Peaks' underground civilization together — dwarves with their hands, gnomes with their records; respect is mutual, though the relationship has evolved as gnomish independence grew |
| **Humans** | Variable; gnomes in the Capital operate comfortably among humans; gnomes in the Iron Peaks have less daily contact; individual relationships are common, cultural fluency requires effort |
| **Halflings** | Generally positive; halfling trading networks move information gnomes want; gnomish archives document things halflings need to prove; a natural exchange relationship |
| **Elves** | Complex; elves carry knowledge in oral and living tradition, gnomes trust only what is written and measured; both find the other's approach baffling and admirable in turns |
| **Mages Guild** | The Guild's archives are partly staffed by gnomish archivists; the relationship is professional and valued on both sides; some gnomes find the Guild's restrictions on certain knowledge frustrating |
| **Adventurers Guild** | Gnomish record-keepers are useful to the Guild for cataloguing dungeon maps, discovered artifacts, and enemy behavior; a practical relationship |
| **Sol Kareth** | Gnomes drawn to Sol Kareth's buried ruins are frequent collaborators with the desert kingdom's own scholars; excavation and documentation go together naturally |

### Tensions and Internal Diversity

**Preservationists vs. Innovators**
The central tension within gnomish culture is between those who believe their purpose is to **record what exists** and those who believe their purpose is to **create new things worth recording**. Preservationists hold that the archive is the goal; the work is in cataloguing, protecting, and transmitting. Innovators hold that a gnome who only copies others has contributed nothing new — that the archive grows only when people add something to it. Most gnomes sit somewhere between, but the tension shapes gnomish institutions and personal identities.

**The Capital vs. the Iron Peaks**
Capital gnomes and Iron Peaks gnomes share the same cultural roots but have diverged significantly. Iron Peaks gnomes tend toward tradition, deliberate pace, and deep specialization; their communities are older, slower, and more insular. Capital gnomes tend toward broader engagement, faster publication, and more political awareness. Each side quietly finds the other somewhat missing the point.

**Knowledge That Cannot Be Shared**
Gnomish culture prizes intellectual honesty — but gnomes who work with the Mages Guild, with royal governments, or with institutions that classify information encounter a recurring dilemma: what do you do when you have documented something that powerful people want suppressed? Gnomish tradition says you make the second copy and keep it safe. What you do with it after that is a matter of conscience.

### Architecture

Gnomish architecture is **compact, organized, and built to last**. The defining feature is **labeling** — everything in a gnomish-built space has a clear designation: rooms, shelves, drawers, individual cabinets. A visitor unfamiliar with gnomish spaces sometimes finds it overwhelming; a gnome who visits a space without labels finds it alarming.

Iron Peaks gnomish communities carve their spaces into the lower mountain stone — smaller tunnels than the great dwarven halls, more tightly organized, with vaulted archive chambers built to resist fire, flooding, and seismic activity. The most important rooms in any gnomish community are the archive vaults.

Capital gnomish neighborhoods are built above ground but carry the same organizational instinct — narrow townhouses with purpose-built storage, workshop spaces that double as lecture rooms, and a communal archive building that anchors the community spatially and socially.

## Non-Playable Intelligent Races

Some intelligent peoples exist within the world but are **not playable**.

| Race | Status |
|---|---|
| **Goblins** | Confirmed non-playable; three named tribes (Stonefang, Mossroot, Ashfire); intelligent, political, capable of diplomacy |

Additional intelligent races may be introduced in future worldbuilding if they naturally fit the setting.

### On Goblins Specifically

Goblins are intelligent people — not mindless enemies.

They have tribal politics, territorial interests, traditions, and the capacity for negotiation, trade, and alliance. See [`docs/world/WORLD_BIBLE.md — Goblin Tribes`](WORLD_BIBLE.md) for the three confirmed tribes and [`docs/world/WORLD_BIBLE.md — Goblin Diplomacy`](WORLD_BIBLE.md) for the diplomacy framework.
