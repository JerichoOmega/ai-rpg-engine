# LIVING_WORLD_DESIGN_PILLAR.md

> **Canon Status:** [CONFIRMED — established January 2026]
> **Authority:** Official design pillar of the project. Governs how every settlement, NPC, and cultural expression is authored across every civilization in Elyndor.
> **Rule:** No settlement, faction, or NPC roster may be authored without applying the principles in this document.

---

## The Pillar

**The world exists independently of the player.**

Players should feel they are visiting a living civilization — not entering a quest hub that switched on when they arrived. History was happening before them. Life continues when they leave. Every alliance, conflict, and tragedy carries weight because the people involved had lives before the story asked anything of them.

This is one of the project's core design pillars. It stands alongside:

- **Handcrafted First** — AI enhances; it never replaces authored content.
- **Difficulty by Choice** — the world's danger evolves with the player's decisions.
- **Offline First** — the complete game is playable without any online layer.
- **Origin Characters** — every companion is also a playable protagonist.
- **Living World** — this document.

---

## Core Principles

### 1. NPCs Have Daily Routines

Every named NPC has a life outside of the player. They eat, sleep, work, socialize, argue, mourn, celebrate. The player intersects with an ongoing routine, not a stationary quest-giver.

### 2. Settlements Feel Inhabited

A settlement is not the sum of its shops. It is a community of people who know each other, hold histories together, and organize their days around one another.

### 3. Families Exist

Households, extended families, adopted kin, refugee-formed families — every settlement has visible family structures. Children live with adults. Grandparents live with grandchildren. Widows and widowers are still part of households. Orphans have foster-homes. Nobody is alone unless their story requires it.

### 4. Children Behave Like Children

Children play, learn, and mimic adult work. Their play reflects their culture. A goblin child practices negotiation; a Halfling child practices meal-sharing; a Dwarven child practices maker's-marks on scrap wood. Children are visible, active, and never merely set-decoration.

### 5. Elders Remain Active

Elders are not put away. They teach, mediate, hold oral history, serve on Council, supervise apprentices, resolve disputes. When an elder dies, the community shows the absence — an empty Council seat during mourning; a workshop with no one at the anvil; a story circle missing its usual voice.

### 6. Ordinary People Have Ordinary Problems

Not every quest saves the world. A child's lost keepsake matters. A neighbor dispute matters. A missing animal companion matters. Small stories build emotional investment.

### 7. History Is Visible in the Environment

History is *shown*, not explained. Walls, tools, trees, roads, and buildings carry the weight of what came before them. A careful player learns as much from what they see as from what NPCs tell them.

### 8. Every Settlement Has a Past, a Present, and a Future

- The **past** is visible — old construction, memorials, weathering.
- The **present** is active — routines, work, family life, festivals.
- The **future** is present — apprentices learning, children playing, plans being made for next season.

A settlement that lacks any of the three feels wrong.

## Time of Day

Every settlement changes throughout the day.

### Morning
- Fires being lit at hearths
- Hunters, patrols, and traders leaving
- Markets opening
- Council chambers preparing for the day's business
- Apprentices arriving before their masters

### Afternoon
- Craftsmen working
- Farming, foraging, and construction at their peak
- Trade at its busiest
- Council debates
- Children in lessons or play under adult supervision

### Evening
- Families gathering for shared meals
- Storytelling around fires
- Music, courtship, informal games
- Elders speaking; children joining the adult circle
- Perimeter walkers beginning their shifts

### Night
- Quiet streets
- Guards or perimeter walkers on rotation
- Lanterns lit at doorways
- Most citizens asleep
- Occasional insomniac, night-craftsman, or star-watcher awake

Different tribes and cultures interpret these rhythms differently — see the culture-specific patterns in `GOBLIN_CULTURE.md` §7 and equivalent sections for other civilizations.

## Environmental Storytelling — Show, Don't Tell

The following are examples of showing history through the environment. Every settlement in the game should include at least one storytelling element per category below.

### Repair and Layering
- Old walls repaired after battles from decades ago — visible seams where new stone meets old.
- Buildings expanded over centuries — clear layers of construction technique.
- Roofs replaced twice over the same beams.
- Streets paved and re-paved; older paving stones visible where the newer surface has worn.

### Named Objects
- Family heirlooms still in daily use — the same cooking pot four generations old, the same forge-hammer three smiths deep.
- Named tools passed down through families, each carrying a small maker's-mark.
- Household items with visible repairs — mended pottery, patched cloaks, resharpened blades.

### Memorial Traces
- Memorial stones placed where community members died or where important events happened.
- Empty council seats during mourning — visible until formally refilled.
- Planted trees marking specific losses (see Mossroot planting-groves; see Elven inherited-name orchards).
- Weathered statues that no one alive remembers commissioning.

### Landscape Memory
- Ancient trees older than any living resident.
- Trees planted by previous generations, now shading the descendants who planted them.
- Old carvings on rocks along old trade routes.
- Former battlefields reclaimed by nature — grass over old stone walls, wildflowers where dead once fell.
- Old roads that no longer lead where they used to; ghost routes that end at ruins.

### Ordinary Marks of Ordinary Life
- Children's play-marks on low walls (chalk games, worn corners).
- Adult work marks (blade grooves in workbenches, soot patterns above hearths, wear patterns on flooring).
- Craft residue (metal shavings, wood-chips, thread ends) at workshop entrances.
- Wildlife adapted to the settlement — pigeons at the market, cats in the archives, dogs at the forge.

### Culture-Specific History Signals
- **Goblin settlements:** First Hearth still burning; Council-chamber engravings; named tools; layered architecture with maker's-marks from multiple generations and sometimes multiple races.
- **Human Capital:** bureaucratic waypoints — offices with plaques of former holders, university halls with alumnus banners, the Bridge of Names.
- **Frontier settlements:** watchfires visible tower-to-tower; caravan wear on the trading-post floors; hitching posts polished by decades of use.
- **Elven communities:** inherited-name orchards; libraries with re-shelved sections labeled by the era of the shelving; door lintels carrying names of prior residents.
- **Dwarven halls:** older halls extend downward; chisel styles date each wall; some walls carry marks of goblin and gnome craftsmen alongside dwarven marks.
- **Gnome archives:** the central archive is older than the settlement around it; catalog cards handwritten by chief archivists across generations; certain records marked "SEALED — [Age name]" with no explanation.
- **Halfling villages:** the long table down the main street is a permanent installation, worn to a polish by generations of meals.
- **Orc clan-holds:** blood-tie standards carrying every combatant's mark; forge-hearths with names of past chiefs.
- **Sol Kareth:** inverted daily rhythm shows in the architecture — cool interior halls, exterior walls patterned to catch dusk light, name-markers on newly-named parts of the desert.
- **Vampire houses:** unnervingly normal daily life at the domestic scale — banquet halls set for guests who never arrive; training yards with named targets; archive shelves where specific books have been *un-shelved*.
- **Refugee settlements:** visibly mixed architecture (whatever materials were available); the *Mixing Meal* long table; nobody's original culture on prominent display, but traces of every culture visible everywhere.

## Small Stories

Not every quest should involve saving the world. Every settlement should contain small stories worth telling. Concrete seed ideas:

- A child loses a treasured keepsake.
- An apprentice damages an important tool.
- Two neighbors argue over a shared apple tree, farmland boundary, or well access.
- A hunter's animal companion goes missing.
- A merchant needs an escort.
- A family requests help preparing for a festival.
- An elder is deciding whether to step down from Council.
- A young craftsman wants to leave the settlement to learn elsewhere; their family disagrees.
- A widow tends a grave she cannot bring herself to plant a tree over.
- A stranger passing through asks for a night's shelter and is not entirely who they seem — but not in a dangerous way. Just human.

These stories should exist in every settlement, not only in the ones the main plot requires the player to visit.

## Universal Application

Living World applies to every civilization equally:

- **Humans** — Capital and Frontier both.
- **Elves** — inherited-name culture; long memory; measured pace.
- **Dwarves** — depth as heritage; maker's-mark walls.
- **Gnomes** — archive-centric settlements; Corwin's cultural context.
- **Halflings** — meal-centric communal life; the long table.
- **Orcs** — bled-with-you belonging; Ragash's cultural context.
- **Goblins** — the definitive treatment in `GOBLIN_CULTURE.md`.
- **Beastfolk** — semi-migratory; seasonal settlements.
- **Sol Kareth** — inverted daily rhythm; naming-the-sands tradition.
- **Vampire Houses** — domestic mundanity beneath monstrous politics.
- **Refugee settlements** — civilizations reassembling themselves.

No settlement of any culture is exempt from the pillar.

## The Corruption Under Living World

The Corruption is the central existential threat to every civilization in Elyndor. It is not a race, a nation, or a tribe.

- It infects Humans, Goblins, Elves, Dwarves, Beastfolk, and any future intelligent race.
- It has no civilization of its own. It cannot create. It only twists, consumes, and destroys what civilizations have built.
- Living World makes the stakes of Corruption *legible* — the more alive a settlement feels, the more its loss to Corruption matters.

Canonical framing: *"No race is born Corrupted. Every Corrupted was once someone who chose… or was broken into choosing."*

## Companion Behavior Under Living World

Living World extends to companions. When the party enters a safe settlement, every recruited companion disperses to a location appropriate to their personality, expertise, and evolution state (per D-03 and the pending Settlement Dispersal ruling). Companions are never idle. Talos teaches at the training ground or speaks with children. Torren is at the forge. Eleanor is in the archive or on a rooftop reading. Ragash tends her hounds. Ronan walks patrols. Corwin scouts the settlement's edges. The Community Healer works in the infirmary or coordinates refugee needs.

Companions are not NPCs in these moments — they are companions living inside the same living world the player is visiting.

## Environmental Storytelling Checklist (per settlement)

When authoring any settlement, verify all of the following:

- [ ] At least one visible repair or layered construction element
- [ ] At least one named or heirloom object in visible daily use
- [ ] At least one memorial trace (stone, tree, empty seat, weathered statue)
- [ ] At least one landscape-memory element (ancient tree, old road, reclaimed battlefield)
- [ ] Children visibly playing at culture-appropriate games
- [ ] Elders visibly active in a culture-appropriate role
- [ ] At least three named non-quest NPCs with visible daily routines
- [ ] At least one small story available as an optional side quest
- [ ] Culture-specific history signals from the list above
- [ ] Time-of-day behavior varies morning / afternoon / evening / night
- [ ] Companion dispersal points defined per the seven-companion roster
